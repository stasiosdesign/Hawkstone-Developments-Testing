The conversion of farm buildings, not just barns

Rural conversions are rarely only about the classic threshing barn. Across the three counties we work with:

  • 01 Traditional barns

    brick, stone and timber-framed buildings whose character is the reason a conversion is worth doing.

  • 02 Stables, cart sheds and outbuildings

    often smaller, often in groups, and frequently the best candidates for a single home or an annexe.

  • 03 Farmsteads and steadings

    ranges of buildings around a yard, where the question is as much which buildings to convert as how.

  • 04 Modern steel-framed and block agricultural buildings

    less obvious candidates, but convertible under Class Q where the structure and setting allow.

  • 05 Farm diversification and glamping schemes

    holiday accommodation and new uses for buildings a working farm no longer needs.

If your building isn't on that list, it may still be a conversion project. That is what the feasibility consultation is for.

Barn conversion planning permission: the two routes

Almost every rural conversion in England takes one of two planning routes. Establishing which one suits your building is the first thing we do.

01

Class Q barn conversions

Class Q permitted development allows certain agricultural buildings to become homes through a prior approval process rather than a full planning application. It carries conditions on the building's agricultural use history, its size, its location and — above all — whether it can be converted without effectively being rebuilt. It is not available for listed buildings, and it is excluded in some designated areas including conservation areas and National Parks.

Where Class Q applies, we assess the building against the criteria, prepare the drawings and supporting information, and manage the prior approval application. Where it doesn't quite fit, you get straight barn conversion planning advice early — not after a refusal.

02

Full planning permission

A full application is the route when Class Q isn't available, when the building has already been altered, or — often — when a better scheme is possible than Class Q permits: a more generous extension, different openings, a considered layout across a group of buildings. It means arguing the case against local policy on rural housing, design and landscape, which is where a design-led approach earns its keep.

Barn conversions in the Green Belt

A large share of the buildings we're asked about sit in the Green Belt. That does not rule out conversion — re-using permanent, substantial buildings can be acceptable in principle — but it shapes what can be added and how the case is made.

Conversion or rebuild? Establishing what your building can take

The most common reason a barn conversion fails is that the building cannot actually support one. So we start there.

  • Structure. Class Q expects the existing building to be capable of conversion without significant new structural elements. A barn needing new foundations, new walls or a new roof frame to stand up is, in planning terms, a rebuild — a different argument and a different route. We look at this honestly at the outset, because everything downstream depends on it.

  • Surveys. Most projects need a measured survey to design from and a structural survey to confirm condition. Because bats roost in agricultural buildings, an ecology survey is routinely required, and it can only be carried out at certain times of year — which affects your programme. Depending on the site, drainage, flood risk, contamination or highways information may also be needed.

  • Access and services. How you reach the building, and how water, power and drainage reach it, are questions planning officers ask and that shape the budget.

  • If it doesn't qualify. Then we tell you what would — a full planning application, a different building on the site, or in some cases a replacement dwelling — and what each involves. The point of a feasibility stage is to make that decision before you have paid for the wrong drawings.

Barn conversion design: keeping the character, changing how it works

Good rural architecture keeps what makes a building worth keeping — the proportions, the masonry, the roof form, the rhythm of the openings — and changes how it works inside. Barns are long, tall and often deep: a gift for volume, a challenge for daylight and layout. Our design effort goes into bringing light into the plan, arranging rooms so the original form still reads, and making new openings sparingly and deliberately.

Where the planning route allows an extension, we design it to be clearly of its own time and clearly secondary to the original building. Where it doesn't, we make the existing envelope do the work.

Barn conversion questions we're asked most

Do I need planning permission for a barn conversion?

Yes, in one of two forms. Some agricultural buildings can be converted under Class Q permitted development, which requires prior approval from the council rather than full planning permission. Buildings that don't meet the Class Q criteria, or schemes going beyond what Class Q allows, need a full planning application. Establishing which applies to your building is the first thing we do.

Does my barn qualify for Class Q?

It depends on the building's agricultural use history, its size, where it is, and whether it can be converted without major structural rebuilding. Listed buildings are excluded, as are some designated areas including conservation areas and National Parks. Our Class Q guide sets out the criteria in full; a feasibility consultation tells you how your building measures up. Class Q guide

What if my barn doesn't qualify for Class Q?

We look at a full planning application, which considers the proposal against local planning policy rather than a fixed checklist. For many buildings that is the better route anyway, because it can support a more generous scheme. In some cases the answer is a different building on the site, or a replacement dwelling.

Can a modern steel-framed or block agricultural building be converted?

Sometimes. Class Q isn't limited to traditional brick and stone barns, so a modern shed can qualify — but the structural test is harder to satisfy and the design needs care to produce a home rather than a clad shed. It's a case-by-case assessment, and one worth making before you buy.

What surveys are needed for a barn conversion?

A measured survey and a structural survey are the starting point. An ecology survey is routinely required because bats roost in agricultural buildings, and its seasonal timing affects the programme. Depending on the site you may also need drainage, flood risk, contamination or highways information. We advise on what's needed and coordinate it.

How much does a barn conversion cost?

It varies more than a new build, because the existing structure sets the starting point: a sound brick barn with good access costs considerably less to convert than a large shed needing extensive repair and new services. Specification, size and the planning route all move the figure. We give budget pointers at the feasibility stage and develop cost control as the design progresses.

How long does a barn conversion take?

Class Q prior approval applications carry a statutory determination period of eight weeks; full planning applications are typically eight to thirteen weeks once submitted, with survey and design work before that. First conversation to planning decision is realistically a matter of months; construction depends on the scale of the scheme. We set out a programme at feasibility stage.

Can I extend a barn as part of the conversion?

Under Class Q, only within limits — the permitted development right allows far less than a full application would. If you want a substantial extension, full planning is the route, and the extension should be designed to sit clearly secondary to the original building.

Is my project a conversion or a rebuild?

This is the question that decides Class Q. If the building can be converted using its existing structure, with repairs and reasonable strengthening, it is a conversion. If it would need new foundations, walls or roof structure to stand, planning authorities treat it as a rebuild — not permitted under Class Q, and requiring a different case under full planning. A structural survey settles it, and we look at it before anything else.

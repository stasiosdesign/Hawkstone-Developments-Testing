Barns, stables, outbuildings and modern farm buildings

“Barn conversion” is the phrase everyone searches, but the work is broader than that. We design conversions of stone and brick threshing barns, Dutch barns, stable blocks and converted stables, farm outbuildings, granaries and whole steadings — and, increasingly, modern agricultural buildings. A steel frame barn conversion or a blockwork shed conversion is often more viable than owners expect: the planning rules do not require a building to be old or attractive, only capable of conversion.

If your building is a redundant farm building of almost any age or construction, it is worth an assessment before you rule anything in or out.

Barn conversion planning permission: Class Q or full planning

Almost every project starts with the same question — do I need planning permission for a barn conversion? There are two routes, and choosing the right one early saves months.

Class Q prior approval

Class Q permitted development allows certain agricultural buildings to be converted to homes without a full planning application. We assess your building against the Class Q criteria, prepare the prior approval application, and design within its limits. It is faster and gives councils less discretion — but the eligibility rules are strict. Our Class Q guide explains them in full.

Full planning permission

Where a building doesn’t qualify — or where a better scheme is possible than Class Q allows — we prepare a full planning application, making the case for conversion on its merits. Many of our strongest projects went this way. Green Belt and countryside policy add constraints we deal with routinely; see our Green Belt guide.

A Class Q refusal, or a building that fails the criteria, is rarely the end. It usually just means the route is full planning, with a design argument to win.

Feasibility before design

The most expensive mistake in this type of project is designing a scheme the building can’t support or the council won’t approve. So we start with feasibility:

  1. Structural condition

    Can the walls, frame and foundations take conversion? Planning law expects a genuine conversion, not a new build barn conversion in disguise — if the building would effectively need rebuilding, the route and the risks change, and you should know that before committing to design fees.

  2. Surveys

    A measured survey to design from, a structural engineer’s appraisal, and where relevant an ecology and bat survey or contamination assessment. We tell you which are actually needed for your building and sequence them so nothing is paid for twice.

  3. Access and services

    Highways access, water, drainage and power — unglamorous, but they decide budgets and sometimes planning outcomes.

This is precisely what our fixed-fee consultation covers: an hour on your building, its likely planning route, its risks and its realistic potential — before you spend anything on design.

How much does a barn conversion cost?

Honest answer: in this region, most full barn conversions land between roughly £2,000 and £3,500 per square metre of finished space, so a typical project runs well into six figures. Averages hide more than they reveal, though — the real cost of a barn conversion is set by the building itself.

The big movers in any cost breakdown are structural condition (underpinning and barn conversion foundations work can dominate a budget), the extent of new openings and glazing, insulation strategy, and services runs to a rural site. A small barn conversion is not proportionally cheap — fixed costs don’t shrink with floor area — while a steel frame barn conversion can be surprisingly economical because the structure is often sound and regular.

Two practical notes. Conversion work can qualify for reduced-rate VAT, which materially changes the numbers — we flag this early. And most lenders will want planning certainty and a structural report before offering a barn conversion mortgage, which is another reason feasibility and planning come first.

We give you a realistic cost picture at feasibility stage, before design begins, and design to it thereafter.

Design that earns the building its second life

The best conversions still read as agricultural buildings. We keep the qualities that made you want the barn — volume, honest structure, long uninterrupted roofs — and solve the hard problems conversion creates: bringing natural light deep into long, narrow plans, insulating to modern standards without suffocating the fabric, and arranging rooms so the building works as a home rather than a compromise.

The result can be restrained and traditional or a fully contemporary barn conversion; several of our projects sit firmly at the luxury barn conversion end of the spectrum. Where the planning route allows, extending a barn conversion is also on the table — a well-judged barn conversion extension can rescue a layout that the original footprint never could.

Barn conversion questions, answered

Do I need planning permission for a barn conversion?

You need either Class Q prior approval or full planning permission — every conversion needs one of the two. Which applies depends on the building’s history and condition; establishing that is the first thing we do.

Does my barn qualify for Class Q permitted development?

The headline tests: agricultural use on the qualifying date, a structure genuinely capable of conversion, and the size and number limits of the Class. The detailed rules are in our Class Q guide — or we assess your building directly at consultation.

What happens if my barn doesn’t qualify for Class Q?

The route becomes a full planning application. That is often an opportunity, not a setback — full planning can permit a better scheme than Class Q’s limits allow.

Can a modern steel-framed or block agricultural building be converted?

Frequently, yes. Class Q does not require a traditional building, and a sound portal frame is a good starting structure. Condition, not age or looks, is what matters.

Is it a conversion or a rebuild?

If the existing structure can’t substantially carry the works, planning treats the project as a new build, and the rules change entirely. Our structural appraisal answers this before you commit.

What surveys are needed for a barn conversion?

Typically a measured survey and a structural appraisal; often an ecology or bat survey; occasionally contamination or drainage assessments. We specify only what your building and council actually require.

How long does a barn conversion take?

As a guide: feasibility in weeks; Class Q prior approval around two months in the system, full planning longer; technical design and construction typically put a whole project at eighteen months to two and a half years from first conversation to moving in.

Can I extend a barn as part of the conversion?

Not under Class Q, beyond its tight limits — but under full planning, yes, where the design justifies it. If an extension matters to you, it can shape which route we recommend.

Do I pay VAT on a barn conversion?

Much conversion work qualifies for the reduced 5% VAT rate rather than 20%. It’s a significant saving that’s often missed; we flag it at feasibility stage so your budget reflects it.

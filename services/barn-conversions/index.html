Barns, stables, outbuildings and modern farm buildings

“Barn conversion” is the phrase everyone searches, but the work is broader than that.

We design conversions of stone and brick threshing barns, Dutch barns, stable blocks and converted stables, granaries, farm outbuildings and whole steadings — and, increasingly, modern agricultural buildings.

A steel frame barn conversion or a blockwork shed conversion is often more viable than owners expect. The planning rules do not require a building to be old or attractive — only structurally capable of conversion. If yours is a redundant farm building of almost any age or construction, it deserves a proper assessment before you rule anything in or out. The first question that assessment answers is which planning route applies.

Barn conversion planning permission: two routes

Almost every project starts with the same question — do I need planning permission for a barn conversion? There are two routes, and choosing correctly at the start saves months.

01

Class Q prior approval

Class Q permitted development allows certain agricultural buildings to be converted to homes without a full planning application. We assess your building against the Class Q criteria, prepare the prior approval application and design within its limits. It is faster and gives the council less discretion — but the eligibility rules are strict.

Our Class Q guide explains them in full →

02

Full planning permission

Where a building doesn’t qualify — or where a better scheme is possible than Class Q allows — we prepare a full planning application and make the case for conversion on its merits. Many of our strongest projects went this way. Green Belt and countryside policy add constraints we deal with routinely; see our Green Belt guide.

A failed Class Q assessment is rarely the end

It usually just means the route is full planning, with a design argument to win — and that argument is our job.

Feasibility before design

The most expensive mistake in this kind of project is designing a scheme the building can’t support or the council won’t approve. So we start with feasibility.

  • Structural condition. Can the walls, frame and foundations take the conversion? Planning law expects a genuine conversion — if the building would effectively need rebuilding, you’re into new build barn conversion territory, where the rules and risks change entirely. You should know that before committing to design fees.

  • Surveys. A measured survey to design from, a structural engineer’s appraisal, and — where relevant — ecology and bat surveys or a contamination assessment. We tell you which your building and council actually require, and sequence them so nothing is paid for twice.

  • Access and services. Highways access, water, drainage, power, and any barn conversion foundations work. Unglamorous, but these decide budgets and occasionally planning outcomes.

This is exactly what our fixed-fee consultation covers: an hour on your building, its likely planning route, its risks and its realistic potential — before you spend anything on design.

How much does a barn conversion cost?

An honest answer: in this region most full conversions land between roughly £2,000 and £3,500 per square metre of finished space, so a typical project runs well into six figures. Averages hide more than they reveal, though — the real barn conversion cost is set by the building itself.

The big movers in any cost breakdown are structural condition, the extent of new openings and glazing, the insulation strategy, and service runs to a rural site. A small barn conversion is not proportionally cheap, because fixed costs don’t shrink with floor area — while a steel frame barn conversion cost can be surprisingly reasonable, since the structure is often sound and regular.

Two practical notes. Much conversion work qualifies for reduced-rate VAT, which materially changes the numbers, and we flag it early. And most lenders want planning certainty and a structural report before offering a barn conversion mortgage — another reason feasibility and planning come first.

We give you a realistic cost picture at feasibility stage, before design begins, and we design to it thereafter.

Design that earns the building its second life

The best conversions still read as agricultural buildings. We keep the qualities that made you want the barn — volume, honest structure, long uninterrupted rooflines — and solve the problems conversion creates: bringing natural light deep into long, narrow plans; insulating to modern standards without suffocating historic fabric; arranging rooms so the building works as a home rather than a compromise.

The result can be restrained and traditional or a fully contemporary barn conversion — several of our projects sit firmly at the luxury barn conversion end of the spectrum. And where the planning route allows it, extending a barn conversion is on the table too: a well-judged barn conversion extension can rescue a layout the original footprint never could.

Barn conversion questions, answered

Do I need planning permission for a barn conversion?

You need either Class Q prior approval or full planning permission — every conversion needs one of the two. Which applies depends on your building’s history and condition, and establishing that is the first thing we do.

Does my barn qualify for Class Q permitted development?

The headline tests: agricultural use on the qualifying date, a structure genuinely capable of conversion, and the size and unit limits of the Class. The detailed rules are in our Class Q guide — or we assess your building directly at consultation.

What happens if my barn doesn’t qualify for Class Q?

The route becomes a full planning application. That is often an opportunity rather than a setback — full planning can permit a better scheme than Class Q’s limits allow.

Can a modern steel-framed or block agricultural building be converted?

Frequently, yes. Class Q does not require a traditional building, and a sound portal frame is a good starting structure. Condition, not age or appearance, is what matters.

Is it a conversion or a rebuild?

If the existing structure can’t substantially carry the works, planning treats the project as a new build and the rules change entirely. Our structural appraisal answers this before you commit to anything.

How long does a barn conversion take?

As a guide: feasibility within weeks; Class Q prior approval around two months in the system, full planning longer; then technical design and construction. Most whole projects run eighteen months to two and a half years from first conversation to moving in.

Can I extend a barn as part of the conversion?

Not under Class Q, beyond its tight limits — but under full planning, yes, where the design justifies it. If an extension matters to you, that can shape which route we recommend.

Do I pay VAT on a barn conversion?

Much conversion work qualifies for the reduced 5% VAT rate rather than 20%. It is a significant saving that is often missed; we flag it at feasibility stage so your budget reflects it.

Do I need an architect for a barn conversion?

There is no legal requirement to appoint one — “architect” is a protected title in the UK, but planning and building work does not demand it. What a conversion needs is design and planning expertise in this specific building type.

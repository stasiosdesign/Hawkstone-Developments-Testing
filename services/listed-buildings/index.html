The buildings we work with

Protection comes in several forms, and the type applying to your property changes what you need to apply for and what evidence has to sit behind it. These are the situations we work in most often.

Listed buildings

Grade I, Grade II* and Grade II properties, where the listing covers the whole building inside and out, along with fixtures and often structures within the curtilage. Almost all of our listed work is Grade II.

Homes in conservation areas

Undesignated houses within a designated area, where the character of the wider street and the setting carry weight, and where permitted development rights are usually restricted.

Locally listed and non-designated heritage assets

Buildings identified by the local authority as locally important without being nationally listed. They do not need Listed Building Consent, but their significance is a material planning consideration.

Buildings affecting a heritage setting

Properties that are not designated themselves but sit close enough to a listed building or historic landscape that the proposal is judged on its effect on that setting.

If you are not certain which of these applies to your property, that is a normal starting point. Establishing designation, significance and constraint is the first thing we do.

Extending a listed building

A listed building extension is not prohibited. It is assessed. The question a conservation officer asks is whether the addition harms the significance of the building, and whether any harm is outweighed by the benefit of keeping the property in active use. That is a case you build with evidence, not a box you tick.

In practice, the schemes that succeed tend to do the same things. They attach to the least significant part of the building, often a later addition rather than the historic core. They are subordinate in scale. They use a limited palette of materials chosen to sit alongside the original rather than imitate it. And they are reversible enough that the historic fabric could, in principle, be returned to its earlier state.

Contemporary extensions are frequently the easier case to argue, not the harder one. A clearly modern addition is legible as new work and does not confuse the historic record, which is precisely what national policy and most conservation officers prefer to a convincing pastiche.

Listed building renovation and repair

Most listed building renovation projects are trying to resolve the same tension: the building needs to work as a modern home, and the things that make it worth protecting need to survive that process. Layout, light, insulation, heating, bathrooms and kitchens all have to be introduced into a structure that was not designed for them.

Our approach starts with understanding what actually carries significance in the building, because that is what determines where you have latitude and where you do not. Later partitions, twentieth-century alterations and unsympathetic previous work often give far more room to move than owners expect. The historic fabric that matters is then repaired and specified properly rather than replaced.

Work this typically includes

  • Reordering internal layouts and reinstating lost proportions
  • Repair and restoration of historic fabric, joinery and external elevations
  • Sensitive introduction of insulation, services, heating and glazing
  • Conversion or repurposing of redundant historic buildings and outbuildings
  • Conservation specifications and detailed drawings for the contractor
  • Complete renovation of period properties within conservation areas

Where a property is a period home but is not listed and not in a conservation area, the constraints are different and the project usually belongs with our luxury architecture service instead.

Listed Building Consent applications

Work that affects the character of a listed building needs Listed Building Consent, and most schemes need planning permission as well. The two are separate applications with different tests, and they are usually best submitted together so the authority assesses one coherent proposal rather than two partial ones.

Consent decisions turn on evidence. An application that explains what is significant about the building, what effect the proposal has on it and why that effect is acceptable is a different document from one that simply shows what is being built. Preparing that argument properly is the difference between a straightforward determination and a refusal.

If you want the regulations explained in detail before you speak to anyone, our listed building consent guide covers what needs consent, what does not, and how retrospective applications are handled.

Homes in conservation areas

In a conservation area the building itself may carry no designation, but the area does, and that changes what you can do without permission. Permitted development rights are commonly restricted by an Article 4 direction, which means alterations that would be unremarkable elsewhere, such as replacement windows, render, cladding, roof changes or a modest outbuilding, can require a full application.

What the authority is protecting is character: the rhythm of a street, the materials that repeat across it, boundary treatments, roofscapes and the spaces between buildings. Proposals succeed when they can be shown to sit within that character rather than interrupt it, and that argument is made through the design itself as much as through the supporting documents.

Our conservation area planning guide sets out when permission is needed and how Article 4 directions work.

Our approach

Significance first, then design

Heritage architecture is often treated as a constraint to be managed. We treat it as the brief. Understanding what is significant about a building, and why, is what tells you where the design opportunity actually is, and it is usually less restrictive than owners fear.

That understanding comes first: what survives from which period, what has been altered, what has been damaged, and what the building’s setting contributes. Only then do we develop the proposal. It means the design and the consent case are built together rather than the second being assembled to justify the first, and it is the reason our applications tend to hold up under scrutiny.

We are a design-led practice working on historic buildings, not a restoration contractor. Where new work is the right answer, we design it to be legible as new work, detailed and specified to a standard that sits comfortably alongside what is already there. Where repair is the right answer, we specify repair. The building decides which.

How a heritage project runs

Appointments are tailored to the project, and not every client needs every stage. Some come to us at the earliest feasibility stage, others already hold consent and need the technical information to build.

  1. Initial consultation

    We discuss your objectives, the condition of the building and the changes you are considering, and give an early view on what is realistic.

  2. Surveys and brief

    Measured building and topographical surveys are arranged, alongside any specialist consultants the project requires, with early input from the conservation officer where that will shape the scheme.

  3. Concept design

    The layout, scale and design direction are developed through drawings, and through 3D visualisations where they help you make decisions about a building that is difficult to picture from plans alone.

  4. Applications and design development

    Materials and architectural details are refined, then the planning application, the Listed Building Consent application and the supporting heritage information are prepared, coordinated and submitted.

  5. Technical design

    Detailed construction drawings and conservation specifications are produced, coordinated with structural engineers and other specialists, and conditions attached to the consent are discharged.

  6. Construction support

    Where appointed, we assist with contractor selection, answer design queries and help oversee the work on site so that approved details survive the build.

If you already have consent and need technical drawings, condition discharge information or construction-stage support, we take on that work as a standalone appointment.

Common questions about listed buildings

Do I need a listed building architect?

Most people begin by searching for listed building architects, and what they are usually looking for is someone who can design the scheme and prepare the consent application to a standard the conservation officer will accept. That is the service we provide. Where a project requires an accredited conservation professional, a structural engineer with historic buildings experience or a specialist heritage consultant, we bring them into the team and coordinate their input.

Do I need Listed Building Consent for internal alterations?

Often, yes. A listing covers the interior as well as the exterior, so removing a partition, altering a staircase, changing joinery or opening up a fireplace can all require consent even though none of it is visible from outside. Redecoration and like-for-like repair usually do not. The test is whether the work affects the character of the building as a building of special interest, and it is worth checking before work starts rather than after.

Do I need planning permission as well as Listed Building Consent?

Usually. They are separate applications judged against different tests, and one does not substitute for the other. Extensions, outbuildings and most external changes need both. We prepare and submit them together so the authority is assessing a single coherent proposal.

Can I extend a Grade II listed building?

Yes, subject to consent. Grade II listing does not prevent extension. What matters is where the extension attaches, how large it is relative to the original building, what it is built from and how convincingly the case is made that the significance of the building is not harmed. Extensions to later or less significant parts of a property are considerably easier to argue than additions to the historic core.

Can a listed building have a contemporary extension?

Yes, and a contemporary approach is frequently better received than a historic imitation. National policy and most conservation officers prefer new work that is honestly of its own time and clearly distinguishable from the original, because it keeps the history of the building readable. The requirement is quality and restraint in the detailing, not the use of period materials.

What is a Heritage Statement and do I need one?

A Heritage Statement sets out what is significant about the building and its setting, and assesses the effect the proposal would have on that significance. Any application affecting a designated heritage asset needs one, and its depth should be proportionate to the importance of the building and the scale of the work. We coordinate its preparation through a specialist heritage consultant and make sure the design and the statement say the same thing.

What is a non-designated heritage asset?

It is a building or structure identified by the local authority as locally important, often through a local list, without being nationally listed. Listed Building Consent does not apply, but the significance of the asset is a material consideration in any planning decision affecting it, so the application still needs to address it properly.

What if previous owners carried out work without consent?

This is more common than people expect and it is usually resolvable. The options are typically a retrospective application to regularise the work, a proposal that addresses the unauthorised alterations as part of a wider scheme, or reversal where the work has caused harm. It is worth dealing with, because unresolved unauthorised work will surface during a sale and can complicate any future application.

How much does a listed building specialist cost?

Fees depend on the scale and complexity of the work, the surveys the building needs and how many specialist consultants have to be involved, so there is no single figure that would be honest. What we can do at the first conversation is give you a clear view of the stages your project needs, which of them we would carry out and what a realistic fee range looks like for a project of that type, before you commit to anything.

Listed, locally listed and conservation area homes

Three kinds of designation bring different obligations, and clients often don't know which applies to them until we look:

  • 01 Listed buildings

    Grade I, II* or II. Any work affecting the building's special interest needs Listed Building Consent, inside and out, and the protection covers the whole building rather than just the parts you can see from the road.

  • 02 Locally listed and non-designated heritage assets

    not statutorily listed, but recognised by the local authority. Consent isn't required as such, but the building's significance is a material planning consideration.

  • 03 Conservation areas

    the emphasis shifts to how a building contributes to the character of the area. Permitted development rights are usually restricted, and demolition and tree works are controlled.

We also work on period homes carrying no designation at all — where the constraints are practical rather than statutory. If that's your house, our Luxury Architecture page is the better starting point.

Listed Building Consent applications: how the process works

Consent is a process, not a form. What follows is the shape of it, and what we prepare at each stage.

01

Listed Building Consent and planning permission

The two are separate. Listed Building Consent covers work affecting a building's special interest; planning permission covers development in the ordinary sense. An extension to a listed house typically needs both, submitted together. Internal alterations may need consent alone. Establishing which applies is the first thing we do, because it sets the programme and the cost.

02

Heritage statements and supporting information

Applications affecting a designated asset need a written assessment of the building's significance and how the proposal affects it. A heritage statement isn't a formality — it's the document that makes the case, and a weak one is a common reason for refusal. We prepare it as part of the application, commissioning a heritage consultant where a building's complexity warrants it.

03

Conservation officers and pre-application advice

Most local authorities offer pre-application advice, and on heritage work it is usually worth taking. Engaging the conservation officer early — with drawings that show we've understood the building — turns the application into a conversation rather than a gamble. We handle that engagement.

Extending and renovating a Grade II listed home

Most of the heritage work we're asked about falls into four kinds:

  • Extensions. The question is rarely whether you can extend a listed building — it's where, how large, and in what language. A well-argued extension that reads clearly as new work often has an easier route than a pastiche addition that confuses the historic fabric.

  • Internal reconfiguration. Removing a partition, opening up a plan, relocating a kitchen or bathroom. Internally, protection is often stricter than owners expect, and the work needs consent even where nothing is visible outside.

  • Restoration and repair. Windows, roofs, masonry, render, joinery. We specify repairs appropriate to the building and coordinate the specialists who carry them out.

  • Full renovation. Bringing a historic house up to modern standards of comfort and performance without stripping out what makes it worth living in — usually the most involved of the four, and the one where design and heritage judgement matter most.

Contemporary extensions to historic buildings

Our approach is significance-led: understand what matters about the building first, then design so that the new work supports it. In practice that usually means new work that is legibly of its own time, sitting quietly alongside the original rather than imitating it — an approach that both conservation officers and the better guidance tend to favour over reproduction detailing.

It also means restraint about what's removed. The parts of a historic house that make it valuable are often the parts an unsympathetic renovation takes out first.

This is where a design-led practice differs from a restoration contractor: we're not only putting a building back, we're deciding what it should become.

Who this service is for

It's for you if:

  • You own a listed building, a locally listed property or a home in a conservation area in Derbyshire, Staffordshire or Cheshire, and you want to extend, alter or renovate it.
  • You're buying a historic property and want to know what's realistically achievable before you commit.
  • You've had a scheme refused, or been told informally that what you want isn't possible, and want a second view.
  • You already have consent and need technical drawings, or need heritage conditions discharged.
  • You've discovered work carried out by previous owners without consent and need to regularise it.

It probably isn't if:

  • Your building has no designation and sits outside a conservation area — see Luxury Architecture instead.
  • Your building is a listed barn or agricultural building. That's a conversion project first: see Rural Conversions.
  • You need a contractor to carry out repairs rather than a practice to design and gain consent for them.

How we work with you

  1. Feasibility consultation. A fixed-fee, 60-minute session with a lead designer, with a desktop review beforehand and a written report afterwards: which consents your project needs, the constraints we can see, and the sensible next step.

  2. Significance and survey. A measured survey to design from, and an assessment of what matters about the building — the foundation of both the design and the heritage statement.

  3. Design and consent. Concept options, then a developed scheme, the heritage statement, and a Listed Building Consent and planning submission that we prepare and manage, including conservation officer engagement.

  4. Technical design. Building Regulations and construction drawings, repair specifications, and coordination of engineers and heritage specialists.

  5. Conditions and build support. Discharging consent conditions, tendering, contractor selection, and oversight through construction.

Listed building questions we're asked most

Do I need Listed Building Consent for internal alterations?

Often, yes. Listing protects the whole building, not just the exterior, so removing a partition, altering a staircase or changing historic joinery can require consent even though nothing changes outside. Routine maintenance and like-for-like repair generally don't. The distinction matters: carrying out works without consent is a criminal offence, not a planning technicality.

Do I need planning permission as well as Listed Building Consent?

Usually, for anything that counts as development — an extension, an outbuilding, changes to the site. The two applications are separate but normally run together. Internal work affecting only the listed fabric may need consent alone.

Can I extend a Grade II listed building?

Yes, in the right place and at the right scale. The test is the effect on the building's special interest, not a fixed size limit. Extensions that sit subordinate to the original, in a considered location, with a clear relationship to the historic fabric, are regularly consented.

Can a listed building have a contemporary extension?

Frequently it's the stronger approach. Guidance generally favours new work that is legible as new over reproduction detailing that blurs what's original. It has to be done well — a contemporary addition that ignores the building is no more likely to succeed than a poor pastiche.

What is a heritage statement and do I need one?

It's a written assessment of the building's significance and how your proposal affects it. Applications affecting a designated asset need one, and its quality materially affects the outcome. We prepare it as part of the application.

Do I need planning permission in a conservation area?

Often, for work that would be permitted development elsewhere. Conservation area designation commonly restricts those rights, and controls demolition and works to trees. The character of the area, and your building's contribution to it, become material considerations.

What is a non-designated heritage asset or a locally listed building?

A building the local authority recognises as having local significance without statutory listing. Listed Building Consent isn't required, but the building's significance is weighed in any planning decision — so the design case still matters.

Can I extend or alter a historic house in the Green Belt?

Possibly. Green Belt policy limits extensions to existing dwellings by scale, and where the building is also listed the two sets of constraints have to be satisfied together. It's a case-by-case assessment.

What if previous owners made unauthorised alterations?

It's common, and it's usually resolvable. Depending on what was done and when, the route may be a retrospective consent application or a negotiated remedy with the conservation officer. It's better addressed deliberately than discovered during a sale.

Can you produce technical drawings and discharge heritage conditions?

Yes — including where consent was obtained by someone else. Post-consent work is a service in its own right, and a common reason clients come to us mid-project.

Getting planning for a barn conversion

Barn conversion planning permission: two routes to consent

Almost every rural conversion in England goes down one of two planning routes. Which one suits your building is usually the first thing we establish.

01

Class Q barn conversions (prior approval)

Class Q permitted development allows certain agricultural buildings to be converted to homes through a prior approval process rather than a full planning application. It comes with conditions on the building's agricultural use history, its size, its location and — most importantly — whether it can be converted without effectively being rebuilt. It isn't available for listed buildings or in some designated areas, including conservation areas and National Parks.

Where Class Q applies, we assess the building against the criteria, prepare the drawings and supporting information, and manage the prior approval application. Where it doesn't quite fit, we tell you early — straight barn conversion planning advice before you spend money on the wrong route.

Our Class Q barn conversion planning guide explains the rules in detail →

02

Full planning permission

A full application is the route when Class Q isn't available, when the building has already been altered, or — often — when a better scheme is possible than Class Q allows: a more generous extension, a change to the openings, a different layout across a group of buildings. Full planning means engaging with local policy on rural housing, design and landscape, which is where a design-led approach earns its keep.

Buildings in the Green Belt

A large share of the barns we're asked about sit in the Green Belt. That doesn't rule out conversion — the re-use of permanent, substantial buildings can be acceptable — but it does shape what can be added and how the scheme is argued.

Can your building be converted?

Feasibility first — before you commit to drawings

The single most common reason a barn conversion fails is that the building can't actually support one. So we start there.

  • Structure.

    Class Q in particular expects the existing building to be capable of conversion without significant new structural elements. A barn that needs new foundations, new walls or a new roof frame to stand up is, in planning terms, a rebuild — and a rebuild needs a different argument and a different route. We look at this honestly at the outset.

  • Surveys.

    Most projects need a measured survey to work from and a structural survey to confirm condition. Because bats roost in agricultural buildings, an ecology survey is routinely required, and its timing can affect your programme. Depending on the site, drainage, flood risk, contamination and highways information may also be needed.

  • Access and services.

    How you'll get to the building, and how water, power and drainage will reach it, are practical questions that planning authorities ask and that shape the budget.

  • What if it doesn't qualify?

    Then we tell you what would — a full planning application, a different building on the site, or in some cases a replacement dwelling — and what each is likely to involve. The point of a feasibility stage is to make that decision before you've spent money on the wrong route.

Our design approach

Barn conversion design: keeping the character, changing the way it works

Good rural architecture keeps what makes the building worth keeping — the proportions, the masonry, the roof form, the openings — and changes the way it works inside. Barns are long, tall and often deep, which is a gift for volume and a challenge for light and layout. We spend our design effort on bringing daylight into the plan, arranging rooms so the building's form still reads, and using new openings sparingly and deliberately.

Where the planning route allows an extension, we design it to be clearly of its time and clearly secondary to the original building. Where it doesn't, we make the existing envelope do the work.

Frequently asked questions

Barn conversion questions we're asked most

Do I need planning permission for a barn conversion?

Yes — in one of two forms. Some agricultural buildings can be converted under Class Q permitted development, which needs prior approval from the council rather than full planning permission. Buildings that don't meet the Class Q criteria, or schemes that go beyond what Class Q allows, need a full planning application. Establishing which route applies is the first thing we do.

Does my barn qualify for Class Q?

It depends on the building's agricultural use history, its size, where it is, and whether it can be converted without major structural rebuilding. Listed buildings and buildings in some designated areas — conservation areas and National Parks among them — are excluded. Our Class Q guide sets out the criteria; a feasibility consultation tells you how your building measures up. Class Q guide

What happens if my barn doesn't qualify for Class Q?

Then we look at a full planning application, which considers the proposal against local planning policy rather than a fixed set of criteria. For many buildings that's actually the better route, because it can allow a more generous scheme. In some cases the answer is a different building on the site, or a replacement dwelling.

Can a modern steel-framed or block agricultural building be converted?

Sometimes. Class Q isn't limited to traditional brick and stone barns, so a modern shed can qualify if it meets the criteria — but the structural test and the design expectations are harder to satisfy, and the result needs careful handling to feel like a home rather than a clad shed. It's a case-by-case assessment.

What surveys are needed for a barn conversion?

A measured survey and a structural survey are the starting point. An ecology survey is routinely required because bats roost in agricultural buildings, and it can only be done at certain times of year, which affects the programme. Depending on the site you may also need drainage, flood risk, contamination or highways information. We advise on what's needed and coordinate it.

How much does a barn conversion cost?

It varies more than for a new house, because the existing structure sets the starting point: a sound brick barn with good access costs far less to convert than a large shed needing new services and extensive repair. Specification, size and the planning route all move the figure. We give budget pointers at the feasibility stage and develop cost control through the design.

How long does a barn conversion take?

Class Q prior approval applications have a statutory determination period of eight weeks; full planning applications are typically eight to thirteen weeks once submitted, with design and survey work before that. Realistically, first conversation to planning decision is a matter of months, and construction depends on the scale of the scheme. We set out a programme at the feasibility stage.

Can I extend a barn as part of the conversion?

Under Class Q, only in a limited way — recent changes allow a modest single-storey rear extension in some circumstances, but nothing more ambitious. If you want a substantial extension, full planning is the route, and the extension needs to be designed to sit clearly secondary to the original building.

Is my project a conversion or a rebuild?

This is the question that decides Class Q. If the building can be converted using its existing structure, with repairs and reasonable strengthening, it's a conversion. If it would need new foundations, walls or roof structure to stand up, planning authorities treat it as a rebuild — which isn't permitted under Class Q and needs a different case under full planning. A structural survey settles it, and we look at it before anything else.
